Tank shapes

Counting water in natural tank shapes

SWOT gives the water height and Sentinel gives the water area. To get the water between two heights, we multiply an area by the height. But which area: the bottom one, the top one, or the average? It depends on the shape of the bank. All numbers here are round, and 1 hectare × 1 metre = 10,000 m³.

The basic idea

A straight slope: layer a and layer b

The water rose from 100 m to 102 m. Sentinel saw 10 ha of water at 100 m, 20 ha at 101 m and 30 ha at 102 m. Layer a is the bottom metre and layer b is the top metre.

Top area × height

30 ha × 2 m = 600,000 m³

Too much. It also counts the sand under the sloping bank as water.

Bottom area × height

10 ha × 2 m = 200,000 m³

Too little. It misses the water lying over the slope.

Average area × height

(10 + 30) ÷ 2 = 20 ha, then × 2 m = 400,000 m³

Just right for a long tank with straight banks, like this one. The sand it wrongly adds is the same size as the water it misses.

Real banks are not always straight. Try five shapes below and see when the average works.

When does the average work?

Real water and three ways of counting with layers, for every shape
ShapeReal waterAverage, one big layerTwo thin layers, a + bFour thin layers, a1 + a2 + b1 + b2
  • Straight banks in a long tank: the average is exact. Around a round pond, straight banks make a cone, and the area grows faster near the top. There the average counts a little too much, and the cone formula below is exact.
  • Curved banks: the average over one big 2 m layer is off by about 10%, or 40,000 m³. Two 1 m layers cut the error to a quarter, 10,000 m³, and four 50 cm layers cut it to a quarter again, 2,500 m³. Each time the layers get half as thick, the error drops to a quarter.
  • Flat shores: the area jumps, but the new water is very shallow, so even four thin layers count 40,000 m³ too much. One more height and area, measured just below the shore, shows where the jump is.

The maths

Integration or differentiation?

Integration: for the volume

The volume is all the thin layers added up, from the bottom to the top: V = ∫ A dh. “Average area × height” is the simplest way to do this sum, and thinner layers make it more exact.

Differentiation: the other way round

How fast the volume grows with the height is the area: dV/dh = A. At 20 ha, every extra 1 cm of water adds 2,000 m³.

The cone formula: for round ponds

Around a round pond with straight banks, the exact sum between two heights is V = H ÷ 3 × (A1 + A2 + √(A1 × A2)). Example: 9 ha at 100 m and 36 ha at 102 m. √(9 × 36) = 18, then 2 ÷ 3 × (9 + 36 + 18) = 42, so 420,000 m³. The average gives (9 + 36) ÷ 2 × 2 = 45, so 450,000 m³: 30,000 m³ too much.

For our project

What this means for us

  • Each day that SWOT and Sentinel both see a tank gives one dot: a height and an area. Many dots give the tank's height–area curve.
  • We add up thin layers along that curve, so curved banks are counted well. More dots mean thinner layers.
  • For our five reservoirs, the Irrigation Department's daily gauge heights give extra dots for free.
  • SWOT's own storage-change numbers use the same two rules: one uses the average area, the other the cone formula.
